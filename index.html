1. People to Know

Know who each person was and why they mattered during World War I.

Austria-Hungary

Archduke Franz Ferdinand

He was the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne. His assassination in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, triggered the chain of events that began World War I.

United States

Woodrow Wilson

U.S. president during the war. He initially kept the United States neutral, asked Congress to declare war in April 1917, and later proposed the Fourteen Points.

United States

Herbert Hoover

Led the U.S. Food Administration during the war. He encouraged Americans to conserve food and reduce waste so more supplies could be sent to Allied forces.

Russia

Czar Nicholas II

Russia’s ruler when the war began. Military defeats and unrest weakened his government, and he abdicated in 1917 during the Russian Revolution.

Russia

Vladimir Lenin

Bolshevik revolutionary leader who took power in Russia in 1917. His government withdrew Russia from the war by making peace with the Central Powers in 1918.

Germany

Kaiser Wilhelm II

Germany’s emperor during most of the war. He abdicated in November 1918 as Germany’s monarchy collapsed near the end of the war.

United States

General John J. Pershing

Commander of the American Expeditionary Forces (AEF) in Europe. He led U.S. troops after America entered the war.

2. Vocabulary & Terms

Use the search box to find a word. Definitions are written in straightforward study language.

AllianceAn agreement between countries to support or defend one another.
MilitarismBuilding up a nation’s armed forces and treating military power as important.
NationalismStrong pride in and loyalty to one’s nation; it can also create rivalry between nations.
ImperialismExtending a country’s power by controlling other lands, peoples, or resources.
U-BoatA German military submarine used to attack ships, including merchant and passenger vessels.
Trench warfareA type of fighting in which opposing armies defend long networks of dug-out trenches.
StalemateA situation in which neither side can make enough progress to defeat the other.
No Man’s LandThe dangerous area between opposing trench lines.
DogfightAn aerial battle between fighter aircraft.
The LusitaniaA British passenger liner sunk by a German U-boat in May 1915. The attack killed 1,198 people, including 128 Americans, and increased U.S. anger toward Germany.
NeutralityNot taking either side in a conflict or war.
Zimmermann TelegramA secret 1917 German message proposing that Mexico ally with Germany if the United States entered the war; Germany suggested Mexico could try to recover territory it had lost to the U.S.
Four Minute MenVolunteers who gave short patriotic speeches, often during movie intermissions, to encourage support for the war.
Food AdministrationThe U.S. agency led by Herbert Hoover that encouraged food conservation and organized food supplies during the war.
PropagandaInformation, often one-sided or emotionally persuasive, designed to influence what people think or do.
Selective Service ActThe 1917 law that required men in specified age groups to register for a military draft.
Committee on Public Information (CPI)A U.S. government agency led by George Creel that publicized the American war effort and built public support for the war.
War Industries BoardA U.S. agency that coordinated wartime production and helped direct the flow of materials and supplies to industries.
AbdicateTo give up a position of power, especially a throne.
ArmisticeAn agreement to stop fighting. The armistice ending the fighting in World War I took effect on November 11, 1918.
The Fourteen PointsPresident Wilson’s 1918 peace plan. It called for ideas such as open diplomacy, reduced trade barriers, arms reduction, national self-determination, and a League of Nations.
The Big FourThe four major Allied leaders at the Paris Peace Conference: Woodrow Wilson (U.S.), David Lloyd George (Britain), Georges Clemenceau (France), and Vittorio Orlando (Italy).
Self-determinationThe idea that people should have a say in choosing their own government and political future.
ReparationsPayments demanded from a country to compensate for damage caused by a war.

4. Six Types of Propaganda

A propaganda poster tries to influence an audience. Identify the technique by asking how the message persuades people.

1

Testimonial

Uses a respected or famous person—or an ordinary person presented as trustworthy—to endorse an idea or product.

Example: A well-known athlete tells people to support a war effort.

2

Name-calling

Uses insulting or negative labels to make an opponent seem bad without fairly explaining the issue.

Example: Calling the enemy cruel names to create hatred.

3

Bandwagon

Suggests that everyone is doing something, so you should join too.

Example: “Everyone is helping—do your part!”

4

Fear

Uses a threat or frightening possibility to pressure people into acting.

Example: A poster warns that the country is in danger unless people support the war.

5

Card-stacking

Shows only selected facts that support one side while leaving out important information that could weaken the argument.

Example: A poster lists only an enemy’s wrongdoings and ignores other facts.

6

Word magic

Uses positive, emotionally powerful words or slogans to make an idea sound appealing without giving much evidence.

Example: “Freedom,” “victory,” or “protect our homes.”

How to analyze a propaganda poster

  1. Audience: Who is the poster trying to reach?
  2. Purpose: What does it want people to think, feel, or do?
  3. Technique: Which of the six methods is being used? Look at both words and images.
  4. Evidence: Point to a specific word, slogan, person, symbol, or image that supports your answer.
  5. Message: Explain the poster’s main message in one sentence.
A poster can use more than one technique. Choose the strongest one and explain the evidence instead of only naming a category.

5. Major Events in Order

Use this 10-point timeline to practice the sequence of the war.

  1. June 28, 1914 — Assassination of Franz Ferdinand. The heir to Austria-Hungary’s throne is assassinated in Sarajevo.
  2. July–August 1914 — War begins in Europe. Alliances and declarations of war turn a regional crisis into a wider European war.
  3. August 1914 — Germany invades Belgium. Germany’s move through neutral Belgium helps bring Britain into the war.
  4. 1914 — First Battle of the Marne. Allied forces stop the German advance toward Paris; the Western Front settles into trench warfare.
  5. May 1915 — Lusitania is sunk. A German U-boat sinks the British passenger ship, increasing tensions with the United States.
  6. 1917 — Russian Revolutions. Nicholas II abdicates; later, Lenin and the Bolsheviks take power. Russia moves toward leaving the war.
  7. January 1917 — Zimmermann Telegram. Germany’s proposal to Mexico becomes public and increases U.S. anger.
  8. April 6, 1917 — United States declares war on Germany. The U.S. enters the war on the Allied side.
  9. March 1918 — Treaty of Brest-Litovsk. Russia’s new government makes peace with the Central Powers and leaves the war.
  10. November 11, 1918 — Armistice. Fighting stops on the Western Front. The peace treaty is negotiated later.
After the fighting: The Treaty of Versailles was signed in 1919. It formally made peace between Germany and the Allied powers.

Three major reasons were Germany’s unrestricted submarine warfare (including attacks that threatened American ships and lives), the Zimmermann Telegram proposing a German-Mexican alliance, and the growing economic and political ties between the U.S. and the Allied Powers. The U.S. declared war on Germany on April 6, 1917.
German foreign secretary Arthur Zimmermann sent a secret message to Mexico. It proposed that Mexico ally with Germany if the U.S. entered the war. Germany suggested Mexico could try to regain territory it had lost to the United States. British intelligence intercepted the message, and it became public in 1917.
Anti-German prejudice and suspicion increased. Some German Americans faced harassment and discrimination. Examples included pressure to stop speaking German in public, schools dropping German-language instruction, German cultural traditions being discouraged, and German-named foods or places being renamed in some communities.
The military needed to assess and train millions of men. Army intelligence and literacy tests revealed that many recruits had limited reading skills, drawing public attention to illiteracy and strengthening calls to improve education. The exact test or statistic used in your class may be in the slideshow notes.
People were encouraged to conserve food so more could be shipped to troops and Allies. Common efforts included eating less wheat, meat, sugar, and fats; avoiding waste; planting victory gardens; and observing “meatless” or “wheatless” days. Herbert Hoover led the Food Administration.
The plan included open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, fewer trade barriers, arms reduction, fairer colonial arrangements, national self-determination, and a League of Nations to help prevent future wars. Wilson considered the League of Nations the most important part because he believed it could help preserve peace.
Wilson generally favored a peace based on principles and a stable international order, rather than revenge. French leader Georges Clemenceau wanted strong security measures and compensation from Germany because France had suffered severe damage. British leader David Lloyd George also faced demands for German punishment and reparations, though his position was more moderate than Clemenceau’s.
The German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and Ottoman empires collapsed or were dismantled. Russia’s monarchy fell in 1917; Germany’s monarchy ended in 1918. The Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires also broke apart.
Many Germans viewed it as unfair and humiliating. Germany was forced to accept responsibility for the war under the treaty’s war-guilt clause, give up territory, reduce its military, and pay reparations. German representatives had little ability to negotiate the terms.
The treaty was signed on June 28, 1919, by Germany and the Allied and associated powers. The United States did not ratify it: the U.S. Senate rejected the treaty, so the United States did not become a party to it through ratification.
Many senators, especially opponents of the League of Nations, feared Article X could draw the United States into future conflicts without Congress independently deciding to do so. Senators also disagreed over the treaty’s terms and the president’s approach. The Senate did not approve the treaty by the required two-thirds vote.

WWI Vocabulary

These definitions explain what each term means specifically in the context of World War I.

AllianceAn agreement between countries to support and defend each other. Alliances helped turn the conflict in 1914 into a larger European war.
MilitarismThe buildup of a country's military forces and weapons. European nations expanded their armies and navies before WWI.
NationalismStrong pride and loyalty toward one's nation or ethnic group. Nationalism increased rivalries and tensions in Europe.
ImperialismWhen a powerful country extends its control over other lands and people. Competition for colonies increased tensions before WWI.
U-BoatA German submarine used to attack enemy and merchant ships during WWI.
Trench WarfareA style of fighting where soldiers fought from long, deep trenches. It made battles difficult and often caused very little movement.
StalemateA situation where neither side can make enough progress to defeat the other. Trench warfare created a stalemate on the Western Front.
No Man's LandThe dangerous, exposed area between opposing trenches.
DogfightAn aerial battle between fighter airplanes.
LusitaniaA British passenger ship sunk by a German U-Boat in May 1915. Americans were among the civilians killed, increasing anger toward Germany.
NeutralityThe policy of not taking sides in a war. The United States stayed officially neutral until 1917.
Zimmermann TelegramA secret German message proposing an alliance with Mexico if the United States entered WWI. Germany suggested Mexico could try to regain territory lost to the U.S.
Four Minute MenVolunteers who gave short patriotic speeches to encourage Americans to support the war effort.
Food AdministrationA U.S. agency led by Herbert Hoover that encouraged Americans to conserve food so more supplies could go to soldiers and Allied countries.
PropagandaInformation, posters, or messages designed to influence people's opinions and encourage support for a cause or government.
Selective Service ActA 1917 law requiring eligible American men to register for the military draft.
Committee on Public InformationA U.S. government agency that used posters, speeches, films, and publications to build public support for WWI.
War Industries BoardA U.S. agency that coordinated industrial production and directed materials toward wartime needs.
AbdicateTo give up a throne or position as ruler. Czar Nicholas II abdicated in 1917.
ArmisticeAn agreement to stop fighting. The WWI armistice took effect on November 11, 1918.
Fourteen PointsPresident Woodrow Wilson's 1918 peace plan. It included ideas such as self-determination, reduced weapons, and a League of Nations.
Big FourThe four major Allied leaders at the Paris Peace Conference: Woodrow Wilson, David Lloyd George, Georges Clemenceau, and Vittorio Orlando.
Self-DeterminationThe idea that people should have the right to choose their own government and political future.
ReparationsPayments made by a country to compensate for damage caused by war. Germany was required to pay reparations after WWI.
